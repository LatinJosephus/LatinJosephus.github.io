Josephus and His Works

Biography

Flavius Josephus or Joseph ben Matthias (c. 37–100 CE) came from a noble Jewish family in Jerusalem. His father was one of a long line of priests, while his mother was descended from the Hasmonaean royal family (Vita 1–5; BJ 1.3). He was a priest himself (BJ 1.3, CA 1.54), who was appointed general and governor of Galilee in 66 CE after the beginnings of a Jewish revolt against the Romans (BJ 2.568). After his capture by the Roman general Vespasian at Jotapata in July of 67 CE (BJ 3.392–398), he prophesises that his captor will become emperor (BJ 3.400–402). When this comes to pass in 69, Josephus is liberated (BJ 4.622–629), and observes the war from the Roman side, even acting as a mediator between the sides (e.g. BJ 5.362–419). After the conclusion of the main events of the Jewish war, he comes to Rome in 71 (Vita 422–423). There he spends the rest of his life as a client of the Flavian emperors Vespasian, then Titus, then Domitian, whence his moniker “Flavius”.

Josephus was the author of four works: the Jewish War, the Antiquities, his Life, and the Against Apion.

The Jewish War (Bellum Judaicum), c. 75–79 or 75–84 CE

Content. Despite its title, this 7-book work spans the period from the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164 BCE), through the Jewish war proper and the final destruction of the Temple (66–70 CE), and then the breaking of final Jewish resistance, which ends at Masada in 74 CE. The Jewish War includes the triumph celebrated at Rome in 71 CE (BJ 7.123–162), while the latest datable event mentioned is the dedication of the Temple of Peace, in 75 CE (BJ 7.158–162).

Date. Josephus wrote a first version of the Jewish War in Aramaic (BJ 1.3), which is lost, before composing the probably much-altered version that survives in Greek. A first version of this was probably completed between 75 and 79, as Josephus says (Vita 361) he presented the work to Vespasian (d. 79 CE). Some have suggested that book 7 is an addition made under Domitian, as late as 84 CE (see Rocca 2026).

Latin Version 1: De excidio Hierosolymitano (c. 370)

The first Latin version of the Jewish War is often called the “(Ps.) Hegesippus”, because of an erroneous attribution to the Christian author that gained popularity from the mid-ninth century. Before that, it was often explicitly attributed to Josephus, with saint Ambrose sometimes credited as “translator”. It is perhaps better known as the De excidio Hierosolymitano, a version of a title found in some manuscripts.

The Latin rendering is not literal, and it transforms Josephus’ 7-book history into a 5-book account, that has been reshaped along Christian lines. There are extensive additions and excisions. The book is now at pains to underline the suffering and wholesale destruction of the Jews, cast as divine reprisal for their role in the crucifixion of Jesus.

Based on internal clues, it was composed in the later fourth-century CE, likely in 368–375 CE (Pollard 2024). It has been edited many times, first by Jacques Lefèvre D’Étaples and Michael Hummelberg (eds.), Aegesippi historiographi fidelissimi ac disertissimi et inter Christianos antiquissimi Historia de bello Judaico (Paris: Jodocus Badius Ascensius, 1510). Repr. Paris, 1511, 1524. The latest critical edition was completed by Vincenzo Ussani and Karl Mras in CSEL 66 (1932, 1960), which is the version used here.

Latin Version 2: Literal translation in seven books

This literal Latin version was once ascribed to Rufinus, an attribution that is almost certainly false (Ussani 1945 and Levenson and Martin 2016). Its date and origin remain mysterious, though most seem to think it dates to c. 400.

The text was first edited in Johann Schüssler’s 1470 Augsburg edition (ISTC ij00481000). The last edition where the Latin was not “improved” is that of Johann Froben in 1524 at Basel, which descends (for the BJ) from the manuscript Dresden SLUB A.111 (1438).

The Latin text used here, by Edward Cardwell, is based on the bilingual 1635 Geneva edition (Cardwell 1837, x). This is a reprinting of the 1611 Geneva, which was in turn based on the 1544 editio princeps of the Greek and the 1548 Basel edition of the Latin (see Ammann 2020, 199). The last reproduces the Latin text in Sigismund Gelenius’ 1534 edition (Ammann 2020, 180). This last edition was derived from the 1524 Basel edition, corrected against Greek manuscripts (see Gelenius 1534, 545, Ammann 2020, 96–99).

A recent critical edition of book 1 was made by Bernd Bader in 2019.

The Jewish Antiquities (Antiquitates iudaicae), c. 93–94 CE

Content. The Jewish Antiquities, also called the Judean Antiquities, is an immense work in 20 books, that recounts Jewish history from Creation to 66 CE, that is, the beginnings of the rebellion already covered by the Jewish War. It offers a parallel account of events and characters in the Old Testament (up to AJ 13.217) and to some extent the New Testament, notably mentioning Jesus at AJ 18.63 (the “Testimonium Flavianum”) and Jesus’ brother, James (AJ 20.200–203). For this reason, it came to be prised by Christian exegetes.

Date. Josephus had long hoped to write a complete history of the Jewish people, but due to its magnitude, he undertook the Jewish War first (AJ 1.6–7). But it was not published until the thirteenth year of the reign of Domitian, i.e. 93–94 CE (AJ 20.267). Drafts may, however, have been circulating beforehand (see Wander 2024, 75).

Literal Latin translation at the behest of Cassiodorus, sixth century

The Antiquities and the Against Apion were translated together into Latin under the auspices of Cassiodorus, in the mid-sixth century, whether in Italy or perhaps at Constantinople (see Mazzucchi 2017, for the latter). Cassiodorus’ involvement is made clear in his Institutions, an introductory textbook to Christian studies and the liberal arts:

Josephus (almost a second Livy) who composed his books of Jewish Antiquities on a large scale. Father Jerome writing to Lucinus Betticus says that he was not able to translate Josephus because of the size of this prolix work. But I have had him translated into Latin in twenty-two books by my friends, a task involving great labour on their part since he is subtle and complex. He also wrote seven other marvellously clear books on the Jewish Captivity. Some ascribe the translation of this work to Jerome, others to Ambrose, still others to Rufinus. The fact that this translation is ascribed to such men declares the special merits of its composition. (Inst. 1.17.1, trans. Halporn 2004, ed. Mynors 1937, 55)

Here and elsewhere, Cassiodorus speaks highly of Josephus, and notes that he has access to an earlier translation of the Jewish War. It is unclear whether this is the 7-book translation or the DEH. Some have suggested that the comments on the “special merits” of the text could only refer to the elegant style of the DEH (e.g. Schürer 1901, 73).

Scholars are also not agreed about the philological value of the Latin translation. The editor of the Greek, Benedikt Niese, put great faith in it: “Alongside the manuscripts I have surveyed … the ancient Latin version must be used to restore Josephus’ words” (Niese 1887, xxvii, and Niese 1892, xxxix). Lukas has more recently shown that the Latin text can reveal a Greek text that was in some places better than the one we have now, and in some places contained errors shared with the Greek witnesses we know today (Lukas 2022, cxv–cxxi). Nodet, on the other hand, felt that the Latin “tells us little about the archetype of the witnesses, even if it is much older than the known Greek manuscripts” (Nodet 2001, xiv).

The text was first edited in Johann Schüssler’s 1470 Augsburg edition (ISTC ij00481000). The last edition where the Latin was not “improved” is that of Johann Froben in 1524 at Basel, which descends (for the AJ) from Munich Clm 4510 and Berlin SB Lat. fol. 226.

A critical edition of the whole Latin Antiquities was started by Franz Blatt, who only published one volume containing AJ 1–5 (Blatt 1958). Recently, Randolf Lukas has continued the work, with a critical edition of AJ 6–7 (Lukas 2022).

Against Apion (Contra Apionem), c. 94–100 CE

Content and Date. In this 2-book work, composed c. 94–100 CE, Josephus defends Jewish tradition, religion, and culture, proving their antiquity and even claiming that Greek philosophy draws on Jewish precepts. Since only some of it directly addresses Apion, an Alexandrian Greek who had attacked Judaism, it might be better called In Defense of the Jews.

Literal Latin translation at the behest of Cassiodorus, sixth century

The Against Apion was translated into Latin at the same time as the Antiquities, under Cassiodorus. His reference to the Antiquities being “in twenty-two books” must lump the two books of the Against Apion in with the twenty of the Antiquities. However, the Against Apion rarely accompanied the Antiquities in the manuscript tradition: only a few dozen copies survive, against more than 200 copies of the Antiquities.

The first edition was made in 1480 by Ludovicus Cendrata at Verona (ISTC ij00484000). A critical edition was published by Karl Boysen in 1898 for the Corpus scriptorum ecclesiasticorum latinorum (CSEL 37), which is the text given on this website.

The Life (Vita), c. 93–94 CE

Josephus also published an account of his life, which he appears to have included as a sort of appendix to his Antiquities (see AJ 20.262–267). It covers his ancestry (Vita 1–6), his education (Vita 7–12), his visit to Rome in 64 CE (Vita 13–16), the Jewish rebellion (Vita 17–421), concluding with his postwar life in Rome (Vita 422–429).

Unlike Josephus’ other works, the Life received no late antique or medieval translation. Readers who wanted to know more about him needed to turn to short biographical treatments like Jerome’s De viris illustribus (c. 13). It was not until 1548 that not one, but two Latin translations appeared: one by Godefroy Tilmann at Paris, and another by Sigismund Gelenius at Basel (see Ammann 2020, 177–187).
