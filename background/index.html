Background

Josephus and His Works

Biography

Flavius Josephus or Joseph ben Matthias (c. 37–100 CE) came from a noble Jewish family in Jerusalem. His father was one of a long line of priests, while his mother was descended from the Hasmonaean royal family. (Vita 1–5; BJ 1.3) He was a priest himself (BJ 1.3, Apion 1.54), who was appointed general and governor of Galilee in 66 CE after the beginnings of a Jewish revolt against the Romans. (BJ 2.568) After his capture by the Roman general Vespasian at Jotapata in July of 67 CE (BJ 3.392–98), he prophesises that his captor will become emperor (BJ 3.400–402). When this comes to pass in 69, Josephus is liberated (BJ 4.622–629), and observes the war from the Roman side, even acting as a mediator between the sides (e.g. BJ 5.362–419). After the conclusion of the main events of the Jewish war, he comes to Rome in 71 (Vita 422–423). There he spends the rest of his life as a client of the Flavian emperors Vespasian, then Titus, then Domitian, whence his moniker “Flavius”.

Josephus was the author of four works: the Jewish War, the Antiquities, his Life, and the Against Apion.

The Jewish War (Bellum Judaicum), c. 75–79, or 75–84

Despite its title, this work spans the period from the reign of Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164 BCE), through the Jewish war proper and the final destruction of the Temple (66–70 CE), and then the breaking of final Jewish resistance, which ends at Masada in 74 CE. The book includes the triumph celebrated at Rome in 71 CE (BJ 7.123–162), while the latest datable event mentioned is the dedication of the Temple of Peace, in 75 CE (BJ 7.158–162).

Josephus wrote a first version of the Jewish War in Aramaic (BJ 1.3), which is lost, before composing the probably much-altered version that survives in Greek. A first version of this was probably completed between 75 and 79, as Josephus says (Vita 361) he presented the work to Vespasian (d. 79 CE). Some have suggested that book 7 is an addition made under Domitian, as late as 84 CE (see Rocca, 2026).

Latin Version 1: De excidio Hierosolymitano (c. 370)

The first Latin version of the Jewish War is often called the “(Ps.) Hegesippus”, because of an erroneous attribution to the Christian author that gained popularity from the mid-ninth century. Before that, it was often explicitly attributed to Josephus, with saint Ambrose sometimes credited as “translator”. It is perhaps better known as the De excidio Hierosolymitano, a version of a title found in some manuscripts.

The Latin rendering is not literal, and it transforms Josephus’ 7-book history into a 5-book account, that has been reshaped along Christian lines. There are extensive additions and excisions. The book is now at pains to underline the suffering and wholesale destruction of the Jews, cast as divine reprisal for their role in the crucifixion of Jesus.

Based on internal clues, it was composed in the later fourth-century CE, likely in 368–375 CE (Pollard 2024). It has been edited many times, first by Jacques Lefèvre D’Étaples and Michael Hummelberg (eds.), Aegesippi historiographi fidelissimi ac disertissimi et inter Christianos antiquissimi Historia de bello Judaico (Paris: Jodocus Badius Ascensius, 1510). Repr. Paris, 1511, 1524. The latest critical edition was completed by Vincenzo Ussani and Karl Mras in CSEL 66 (1932, 1960), which is the version used here.

Latin Version 2: literal translation in 7 books

This literal Latin version was once ascribed to Rufinus, an attribution that is almost certainly false (Ussani, 1945; Levenson & Martin, 2016). Its date and origin remain mysterious, though most seem to think it dates to c. 400.

The text was first edited in Johann Schüssler’s 1470 Augsburg edition (ISTC no. ij00481000). The most recent complete edition was that of Johann Froben in 1524. The Latin text used here, by Edward Cardwell, is based on the bilingual 1635 Geneva edition (Cardwell, 1837: p. x). This is a reprinting of the 1611 Geneva, which was in turn based on the 1544 editio princeps of the Greek and the 1548 Basel edition of the Latin (see Ammann, 2020: 199). The last reproduces the Latin text in Sigismund Gelenius’ 1534 edition (Ammann 2020: 180). This last edition was derived from the 1524 Basel edition, corrected against Greek manuscripts (see Gelenius 1534: 545, Ammann 2020: 96–99).

A recent critical edition of book 1 was made by Bernd Bader in 2019.

Who Translated Josephus’ Works into Latin?

Cassiodorus (d. 580 CE) was a Roman aristocrat and scholar, who founded a monastery on his familial lands in southern Italy. This monastery was named Vivarium. Around the middle of the sixth century, Cassiodorus wrote the Institutions, a book meant to act as a study guide for monks in his monastery. In it, he speaks highly of Josephus, and mentions commissioning a Latin translation of the Antiquities. Cassiodorus also notes that he has access to an earlier translation of the Jewish War, seemingly made around 400 CE.

Institutions, I.xvii.1 (ed. Mynors, p. 55):
[De Historicis Christianis] . . . Ioseppus, paene secundus Livius, in libris Antiquitatum Iudaicorum late diff.usus, quem pater Hieronymus, scribens ad Lucinum Betticum, propter magnitudinem prolixi operis a se perhibet non potuisse transferri. hunc tamen ab amicis nostris, quoniam est subtilis nimis et multiplex, magno labore in libris viginti duobus converti fecimus in Latinum. qui etiam et alios septem libros Captivitatis Iudaicae mirabili nitore conscripsit, quam translationem alii Hieronymo, alii Ambrosio, alii deputant Rufino; quae dum talibus viris ascribitur, omnino dictionis eximia merita declarantur.

English translation (James and Barbara Halporn)
‘[Christian Historians] … Josephus (almost a second Livy) who composed his books of Jewish Antiquities on a large scale. Father Jerome writing to Lucinus Betticus says that he cannot translate Josephus because of the size of this prolix work. We have had him translated into Latin in twenty-two books by our friends, a task involving great labor on their part since he is subtle and complex. He also wrote seven other marvelously clear books on the Jewish Captivity [= the Jewish War]. Some ascribe the translation of this work to Jerome, others to Ambrose, still others to Rufinus. This work, since it is ascribed to such men, declares the special merits of its composition.’
